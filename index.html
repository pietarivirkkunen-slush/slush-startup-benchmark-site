Slush Startup Report 2026

Slush startups raise more, raise from better investors, and grow faster than European peers

A matched benchmark of 2,108 pre-Series B European startups, founded 2016 or later, against 229,866 peers.

This report benchmarks 2,108 European startups that attended Slush for the first time in 2022, 2023, or 2024 against 229,866 matched peers — controlled for founding year, country, and growth stage. In the period after attendance, Slush companies raised institutional capital at 3.5× the rate of the benchmark. The premium holds across all three cohorts and every region tested, and is largest for previously bootstrapped companies at 3.1×.

The investor quality gap is even wider: Slush companies are 7× more likely to attract a top-0.1% global VC and 4.8× more likely to gain a US investor. Beyond fundraising, Slush companies graduate to the next funding stage at 2.5× the benchmark rate, grow headcount at 1.2×, and acquire non-dilutive grant funding at 3.4×.

The findings are correlational; the benchmark controls for stage, geography, and founding year, but Slush attracts ambitious founders by design.

The fundraising premium

The fundraising premium is real, consistent, and largest for bootstrapped companies

Slush companies raise institutional capital at 3.5× the rate of comparable European peers. That figure comes from tracking 2,108 European companies from the moment they first attended Slush — measuring how many raised a VC round afterward — and comparing them to 229,866 European peers matched on founding year, country, and growth stage. The premium holds in every cohort and every region tested.

The premium differs by starting position. Slush is nearly evenly split between companies with no institutional capital before attending (1,009, or 48%) and those that already had backing (1,099, or 52%). That mix is notable: in the European benchmark, 89% of companies are bootstrapped. Among bootstrapped Slush attendees, the post-event VC rate is 17.00% — against 5.60% for bootstrapped European peers, a 3.1× premium. Among already-backed attendees, the follow-on rate is 35.00% against 23.20% for peers, a 1.5× premium. The door-opener effect is twice the size of the follow-on effect.

The aggregate 3.5× is higher than both subgroup multiples because Slush draws disproportionately from already-backed companies — who have higher fundraising rates in both populations. Both figures are accurate and reported; the 3.1× is the number that speaks most directly to bootstrapped founders.

The premium is broad. It holds in every cohort we measured, every European region, and every industry cluster. Early-stage companies outperform peers at 3.5×; breakout-stage companies at 1.8×. The association is positive across every segment we analyzed.

The premium is not only about frequency — it also shows up in deal size. Among companies that raised post-event, the median Slush round was roughly twice the size of a comparable peer round. This is measured on funded companies only and should be read alongside the rate figures, not instead of them.

The cap table premium

It is not just whether you raise — it is who you raise from

Slush companies are 7.0× more likely than comparable European peers to be backed by a top-0.1% global VC. The overall fundraising premium is 3.5×. The fund-quality premium is twice that. Slush companies are not just raising more often — they are raising from better funds at a proportionally higher rate than the fundraising premium alone would predict.

The premium widens as the tier narrows. A uniform lift across the ecosystem would produce flat ratios; instead, the ratio rises from 5.10× at the top decile to 7.0× at the top 0.1%. The Slush cohort is not randomly distributed across the VC quality spectrum — it is concentrated toward the top of it.

Slush companies attract US investors at 4.8× the rate of comparable European peers. 22% of the cohort have at least one US investor on their cap table, against 4.60% of peers — a 17-percentage-point gap and the widest single-metric difference in this study. The gap likely reflects a combination of access and selection effects: European companies that attend Slush may be better positioned to attract international capital, and the pool of US investors active at Slush is itself selective. The direction of causality cannot be determined from this data alone.

Post-event investor acquisition rates provide the most temporally precise measure. The round is dated after the company's first attendance, and the investor is new to the cap table. On this basis, 29% of Slush companies gained a new investor post-event; 7% gained a US investor; 12.2% gained a top-10% global VC; and 4.4% gained a top-1% global VC.

Growth velocity

The premium extends beyond fundraising

Raising capital is not the only thing Slush companies do faster. The premium shows up in stage progression, team growth, and non-dilutive funding — consistently and across all three cohorts.

Slush companies advance at least one funding stage at 2.5× the rate of peers. 14% of the cohort moved up at least one stage — bootstrapped to seed, seed to early VC, early to growth — against 5% of peers. The graduation premium is widest at the bootstrapped layer, at 3.7× for the 2022 cohort, and narrows as starting stage rises. The pattern mirrors the fundraising premium: it is largest where the starting position is lowest.

Team growth follows the same pattern. 61% of Slush companies with measurable headcount data grew their teams in the observation window, against 49% of peers — a 1.2× premium. This is reported as a directional signal; Dealroom employee data is available for roughly 70% of companies in the population, so the comparison is built on the subset with non-zero values.

Non-dilutive funding compounds the picture. 24% of Slush companies received a grant or support-programme allocation, against 7% of peers — a 3.4× premium. Grant funding is tracked separately from VC to avoid conflating dilutive and non-dilutive capital. For deep tech, climate, and research-intensive companies, non-dilutive funding typically runs alongside equity, not instead of it.

Across every operational measure in this study — fundraising rate, investor quality, stage progression, headcount growth, and grant allocation — Slush companies outperform the matched benchmark. Whether the premium narrows as the 2023 and 2024 cohorts mature is a question this study returns to in its second edition.

Methodology

What we measured, and how

The analysis covers 2,108 European companies that attended Slush for the first time in 2022, 2023, or 2024 — founded 2016 or later, pre-Series B at first attendance. The benchmark is 229,866 Dealroom-tracked European companies from the same vintage and stage, matched on founding year, country, and growth stage. For Slush companies, the observation window opens the day after first attendance. For benchmark peers, it covers the equivalent post-2022 period.

The 2,108 companies are drawn from a paid-ticket universe of 4,838 unique companies across Europe and the US over the 2022–2025 events. Europe-only, 2022–2024 cohorts, founded 2016+, pre-Series B filters reduce that universe to the analysis population.

The 2025 cohort is excluded. Six months of post-event data is not enough to register the rounds that will eventually appear; the measured rate is artificially low. The 2025 cohort enters the analysis in the 2027 edition.

The study is observational, not causal. Matching controls for founding year, geography, and growth stage. It cannot control for the ambition, network, or fundraising-readiness of companies that choose to attend Slush. The premium reflects a combination of who attends and what attending does; the data cannot separate the two.

Definitions

VC rounds counted: Seed, Angel, Early VC, Series A–H, Growth Equity VC, Late VC, Convertible, Private Placement VC. Grants and support programmes are excluded from VC counts and tracked separately. Acquisitions, IPOs, debt, spinouts, and post-IPO rounds are excluded from both.

Funding stage hierarchy (Dealroom classification, used throughout the report and graduation heatmaps):

Bootstrapped / No prior VC — no institutional VC round on record at the time of first Slush attendance. This is a point-in-time classification: a company that raises a Seed round after attending Slush is still counted as bootstrapped at attendance.

Seed / Angel — at least one Seed or Angel round on record at time of first attendance; no Early VC or later stage round yet. The graduation heatmap tracks how many of these companies subsequently reach Early VC.

Early VC — Dealroom's designation for the first institutional lead round, roughly equivalent to a Series A. Includes round types labelled Early VC and Series A in Dealroom. The bootstrapped heatmap panel counts any qualifying VC round (including Seed) as graduation; the Seed/Angel panel uses only Early VC and Series A as the graduation event.

Growth / Late VC — Series B and above, including Growth Equity VC and Late VC round types. Companies already at this stage at first attendance are excluded from the graduation heatmaps but included in all other analyses.

Investor tier ranking (Dealroom power law) — Dealroom assigns a global rank to approximately 40,000 investors based on deal activity, portfolio quality, and exits. For each company, the analysis uses the best rank — the rank of its single highest-ranked investor across all qualifying post-attendance rounds. Tier thresholds are defined as percentages of this ranked universe:

  • Top 0.1% — rank ≤ 40
  • Top 1% — rank ≤ 400
  • Top 2.5% — rank ≤ 1,000
  • Top 10% — rank ≤ 4,000

Approximately 24.5% of Dealroom-tracked investors hold a power-law rank. Companies whose cap tables contain only unranked investors are excluded from tier comparisons; their fundraising activity is captured in the overall backing rate but not attributed to any tier.

Caveats

Dealroom coverage is stronger in Western Europe. This dampens both Slush and benchmark figures in Central and Eastern Europe.

Investor enrichment data is partial. AUM covers 6.5% of investors; power-law rank covers the top 40,000 funds globally (~24.5% of tracked investors hold a rank).

Survivorship. Dealroom under-represents failed companies. This may inflate absolute fundraising rates on both sides but should not materially affect the ratios.

Stage graduation time anchors differ between cohorts and benchmark. For Slush companies, quarters are measured from November 1st of the attendance year: Q4 for the 2022 cohort reaches November 2023; Q4 for the 2023 cohort reaches November 2024. The benchmark is a single pool anchored to November 1, 2022 — every benchmark company's Q1–Q12 starts there, regardless of when it was founded or last raised. When reading the graduation heatmap, a 2023 or 2024 Slush cohort row and the benchmark row therefore cover different calendar windows. The design measures relative pace — how quickly companies graduate compared to a baseline rate — not performance within a shared calendar period.

This is the first edition of a longitudinal study. The 2022 cohort has three full years of post-event data; the 2023 and 2024 cohorts will show higher rates as the data matures. The 2025 cohort enters the analysis in the 2027 edition.
